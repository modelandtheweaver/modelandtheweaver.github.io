A making-of · a music video for Amber & Ink · 6–13 October 2026

While The Ceiling Moves

How a song about a panic attack became a comic book that moves: four days, ten sessions, one kitchen drawn fifty times, and every word on screen at the moment it's sung.

Watch the film on YouTube · 4:08

This is the story of how the video was made: what we tried first, what we threw away, what the song taught us along the way, and how the details became the story. It was made in conversation between , who wrote the song and made it with Suno, and Claude, working in Claude Code, which wrote and ran every script, with Codex drawing the pictures. The song is credited to Amber & Ink, after its two voices: her warm amber, his ink. 's notes appear here as they were given, in warm balloons, the way the film letters the voice that stays.

The shape of the film

One curve drives everything

Before any picture, the film had a shape: a designed "unease" curve from 0 (still) to 1 (the peak), drawn over the song's sections. Every distortion follows it: the room's wobble, the double vision, the glare, the watery warp. It builds through the verses and choruses, peaks in the second instrumental, and drains to nothing across the bridge, so the outro can be perfectly still. Underneath are the seven sessions that built it, one section of the song at a time. Each band links to its part of the story.

Session 1 · 6 October, 1:18 am

The brief

Hello! Something a bit different for us this time. Do you think you could help make a music video for this song? We have Codex available for Imagegen, but I'm hoping to create something programatically using code, SVGs, animation, blender, etc.
, the first message

The song is While The Ceiling Moves: four minutes and eight seconds of pop with a country delivery and arpeggiated banjos, about 132 beats a minute. It's about a panic attack, being talked down by someone who stays, and grounding into stillness. It's a duet: his voice is the man coming apart in the kitchen at night, hers is the one who reaches him.

What came with it was a Suno export: the mixed MP3 (with the lyrics and their section tags tucked into its ID3 tag), ten separated stems from drums to backing vocals, and a MIDI transcription of each. That turned out to be nearly everything a programmatic video needs. The one thing missing was the hardest to replace: Claude can't listen to music.

Session 1 · the first morning

Listening without ears

So the first job was to turn the song into data. The MIDI's tempo map gave a beat grid, once we found that Suno's MIDI runs about 190 milliseconds early on every stem. Each stem got a loudness curve and a list of its hits. Whisper transcribed the isolated vocal stem with a time for every word, and an alignment matched what Whisper heard against the real lyrics, so the words on screen would always be the right ones. 313 of the 360 sung words matched on the first pass; the misses were in the choruses, where backing vocals overlap and lines repeat.

Then the human half. A karaoke check video filled in each word as it was sung, flagging the weak lines, and listened and sent timecodes back:

1:05 "Just leave your hand..." skips over and races to "I'll feel your grip relax m" and then hangs on "my" until we catch up again properly on "fist"
, by ear

Each note became either a pin (a time fixed by ear, never moved again) or a general rule, checked against the version already approved so nothing else shifted. Three rounds later: "Lyrics are good now, start on the Blender kitchen." Everything after this keys off those timings. Later the same stem told us who sings what, by pitch: his voice around 130-210 Hz, hers around 300-350 Hz, which decided which lines are lettered in her warm colour.

Session 1 · a first proposal

A storyboard

The first proposal was a storyboard page of 23 frames drawn by Codex: the camera as the person on the kitchen floor, the room tipping and coming apart as the song builds, his words in cold white and hers in warm amber, and the unease curve falling to complete stillness at the end. It asked four questions. The answer to one of them set a rule the whole film kept:

I feel like all lyrics should be on screen, but not as plain subtitles; woven cleverly into the surroundings, or flashed chaotically around at confusing moments, to suggest the mental storm and confusion

Most of these frames were never built as drawn. Two of them waited two days and came back, changed, at the very end: the breathing square drawn one side at a time, and the film's only wide shot, the two of them on the floor against the cabinets at dawn.

Session 1 · midday to evening

A day in Blender

The first real attempt was a film rendered in 3D. One Python script built the whole kitchen in headless Blender in about three seconds: cabinets, fridge, sink and window, 962 individual tiles, a strip light, a warm hallway and a front door, with white ink outlines. The whole room hung off one pivot so it could tip around a level camera, and one warp field bent walls, floor and ceiling together (: "unmoving walls reads oddly"). A scripted first-person camera looked around the room, and a perception layer added after rendering gave it glare, double vision, flickering edges and tears.

Some of it landed straight away. The count and the fizz ("the count + fizz is very effective") survived into the final film, and so did the glare and the smear. Much of it didn't, and the notes were precise about why:

The ceiling looks more like a disco floor than flickering tiles.
, on the first preview
The lurch is stronger, yes, the motion isn't quite right overall, more like a bobble-head wobble. … I feel more incapacitated than anxious based on the body motions.
, on the third

Each round fixed something (the tip became a dolly-zoom stretch, he stood at the counter before sinking to the floor, his glances darted between the window, his hands and the phone), and each re-render took seventeen minutes. The real problem was steering: a continuous camera in a 3D room is hard to direct in words.

There's a lot I like about this, but I don't know how to describe the changes I want to make very well. I wonder if we should experiment with a different style. I'm thinking that a lyrics focused, comic book style approach might work.
, 7:37 pm

Session 1 · the evening

The comic

A comic suits this song. The lyrics are already a run of small, concrete, observed moments (his hands, the tiles, the phone, the lock, the siren in the rain), and each one is a panel. It also gives a vocabulary for feedback: "this panel's too busy", "this one should hold longer". And the two voices map straight onto comic language: his lines in boxed captions, in his head; her words in speech balloons, spoken to him.

Two mock pages tested the look: one built from our 3D renders with a comic treatment, and the same page with every panel illustrated by Codex.

The method that came out of the mock is the one the whole film used. Our Blender set renders a layout for each panel: the camera, the room, which tiles will glow. Codex redraws that layout as comic art against one style reference, and keeps its composition almost exactly. Because we know where every tile sits in 3D, the animator can put light back over the drawing: the drawing stays still while the light moves.

The first 44-second moving test rendered that evening, in six minutes instead of seventeen.

This is shaping up nicely! The style is great and the format works well; the sequencing is pretty sweet too.

Sessions 1-2 · 6 October, evening to midnight

Sterile or alive

The 3d model we've used to set the scene is minimising the details we're getting … it feels a bit sterile rather than lived in.

The redraws were faithful to a nearly empty 3D kitchen, so Codex drew a nearly empty kitchen. Two drawings it had made freely for the mock felt alive, because it had invented the clutter: a tea towel, a cafetière, photos on the fridge. They were also inconsistent: a different tap and a different room every time, and a tea towel folded "underneath the sink's actual basin, like could never happen!"

's fix: use the 3D model to pin the framing and the consistency, and give Codex reference sheets of the room. We called it the room bible, and it became the spine of the project. It started with questions about the people, and the answers turned a kitchen into a home:

This is a song about being there, and trusting someone when you are at your most vulnerable and out of control. The narrator lives together with his partner, but doesn't know how to cope and has let everything bottle up until he breaks down in panic. This is their family home.

So: a family home at a quarter past three in the morning. A married couple with plain gold bands; their young child asleep upstairs, never seen, present in bright primary colours (handprints on the fridge, a friendship bracelet on his wrist, a red booster seat). No faces, ever: the photos on the fridge show hands, feet and backs. One file, room.json, holds 66 props, each with its place, size and colour, what it is, what it means, and what it must never be ("the tea towel hangs over the oven door's bar handle; never in or under the sink"). Every prop is blocked into the 3D set as a simple box or card, and every redraw's prompt lists exactly the props its camera can see.

From the master came a reference set: the table side and the hallway drawn against it, close-ups of the fridge door and the sink, and a sheet of each person's hands. With no faces, the hands are the characters: his charcoal knit and the child's woven bracelet, her mustard corduroy and a brown leather watch.

Every session

The details are the story

We'll have to watch out for details in these, there are several howlers if we look closely: The cup with two handls, a picture showing three feet in a row, for example. The fridge is the trickiest.

Image models are good at the whole and careless with the small. From the first master plate on, every drawing was checked at full size and zoomed in: mugs, photos, paper, and above all hands. The worst offenders got rules in the bible and the prompts (each mug has exactly one handle; any hand has five fingers). The rest were fixed one at a time with a patch tool: Codex edits the whole picture, and only the boxed area, or only the pixels that actually changed, is pasted back, so nothing else can drift.

Some catches were more than mistakes. They changed what a picture said.

Looking at 14_fist_and_holding_zoom.jpg, his ring is missing but hers is there, which tells a different story.
, session 3

Her boots stood under the coat hooks while she walked in wearing them. She sings "six words arrive", so the six messages must be his, in his cold colour, and the reply typed and erased is hers. A rule came out of each: one thing, one place; and whose colour a thing is in says who it belongs to.

The patch tool also made something new possible. A fist that opens, a window that clears, a room that brightens at dawn: each later drawing was made from the approved one, changing only what had to change, so the two dissolve into each other exactly.

Session 2 · a detour

A print effect, parked

The redrawn room had lost something the first panels had: a comic-print texture Codex had drawn into them. A regular print screen was wrong ("not as simple as a grid of dots"). pointed at the reference: irregular dots whose size follows the shading, small and sparse in the light, big and crowded in shadow. We built it, dots sized by the light's falloff, kept off ink lines and photos.

It works in some places, but not others. It seems painted on in places, rather than part of the composition. I think we should go without for now, since we could spend forever on this detail.

The tool is kept for another day. Knowing when to stop was the useful lesson. The same instinct came back in session 7, when a skin texture did need fixing and a post-process did the job.

Session 3 · after midnight

Words in the room

I think the lyrics can be hidden in the scene, scribbled onto notes and papers; traced in the window's steam. The narrator has trouble expressing himself, and it turns up in the environment.

Codex was never allowed to write: every prompt says paper carries only faint, unreadable marks. The words are lettered by code, in each person's own hand. Fifteen open-licence handwriting fonts were compared. His is a spiky blue biro, hers a round brown felt-tip, and every letter is drawn separately with a little jitter so repeated letters differ. The words write themselves on at pen speed, at the moment they're sung, warped onto the paper in the drawing and multiplied into it so they take its light. In the steam they wipe the mist clear instead, with beads of water running down after each word.

That's so good! Brilliant. Love the extra details and touches; the writing on the window works very well.
, on chorus 1

Sessions 3-7 · 7 October

The song, page by page

From here the film was built one section at a time, each in a fresh session: a proposal built as real output, stills, a test video with sound, 's notes, and rounds until it was right, then a commit and a handoff for the next session. Two to nine rounds a section. Each section has its own pages; together they tell the story of one night.

Chorus 1 0:44-1:16 · session 3

He's on the floor. The ceiling fills the page and moves, softly, then tears into jagged bands on "moves", "lose" and "room". On "lose the room" the whole kitchen drains away to the centre as tunnel vision closes in. The doorway doubles for "twice as loud"; her "I'm here" comes from the phone on his chest.

The first version had her come home here: her key in the lock, the deadbolt turning, her boots stepping in. It worked, and it was too early.

This works really well, but from a storyboard perspective, I think we need her coming home in the final section, not so early in the song.

Instrumental 1 and verse 2 1:15-1:56 · session 4

The quiet after: the call-light under his hand dims, and her list writes itself on, a line to a bar. Then it comes back: the pipes click, a blue siren sweeps the rain, the tiles' fizz crawls down his fingers, the kitchen washes out "a shade too white", and the lock is checked four times, then four. Her voice reaches him a beat late, as squiggles he can't make out ("this is a comic and we don't know the protagonist yet").

The perception layer's watery warp climbs through this verse, and at full strength it melted the drawings and bent the captions.

The warp is quite strong in places, so let's dial it back a little bit otherwise it's going to be too hard to read and concentrate, which is kinda the point, but also don't want to overdo it.

So the curve bends at a knee and keeps building more gently, and the lettering gets its own calm pass: words stay readable while the art swims.

The same words, and everything worse. He has slid down the front door and sits in the hall. The hall ceiling cracks into pieces on "moves" and they drift out into the dark. His heart is the loudest sound in the house. Her "I'm here" is readable now, but the line is breaking up.

That is so good. I love the fractured/ripped image falling apart. I think a horror dolly … down the length of the hallway here would really suit if we could work that in somewhere.

The call drops on "the signal breaks" and the page goes dark, with a ringing in his ears and his pulse on every kick drum. She rings back: "LOOK AT ME, COUNT TO FOUR". He can't stay against the front door (she has to come in through it later), so on "I find the floor beneath the floor" he crawls down the hall to the fridge, to her notes and her breathing card. Then the count. Her card's square is traced round in warm light, one side for each sung line: in, hold, out, hold. The wobble drains away to exactly nothing on "the walls stay still". In the near-silence her voice counts him out from the phone, and "breath begins" prints itself inside the square, whispered by one of them; it could be either.

The final chorus 3:10-3:42 · session 7

The same chorus words turned round. Hushed at first: the ceiling moves again, slowly, breathing with the card's count, and on her "Stay with me" the room drifts back together out of the dark in the pieces chorus 2 broke it into. Then the homecoming saved since session 3: every sound is her coming home. TICK, her key, the deadbolt with his tally on the note above it, the door. "I'm here, I'm here," and his voice joins hers on "we say it out loud".

The notes on this round were all about what details imply:

Why are her knees soaked? A few drops makes sense, but this looks like she's been changing a tyre.
I'm seeing a weird dotty skin texture on the hands quite often … in this final shot it looks definitely generated … like a skin disorder.

Her hand over his fist came next, and the fist unclenching. But the last frame held too long, and remembered the storyboard:

… in our original storyboard we had them sitting in a patch of morning sun, cuddled together against the kitchen cabinets in a peaceful scene. … the entire sequence from her coming in and kneeling down, could continue on to dawn breaking, bringing her colour in and bathing them both.

So the film breaks its own rule once. The camera leaves his eyes for its only wide shot: the two of them on the floor under her coat, his cheek on the top of her head, her face hidden in her hair, checked at twice the size to make sure there are no faces. Dawn comes up through the whole section. Codex relit each drawing, and we kept only its soft light and colour under the original's own lines, so every line stays put and each light dissolves into the next.

The outro and the ending 3:42-4:08 · session 7

I think this rule break works, and ties up the song, but I've realised that I'd got ahead of things, we've now used the final scene.

The storyboard's last shot had been spent, so the ending needed a new idea. We tried the comic closing. On the final crash the page pulls back into a book, and the song's pages turn back over it one leaf at a time, slowly, then faster every bar, so the calm pages can be read and the panic is a blur. Each page is printed whole, every caption lettered. The cover comes over last and slams shut on the last hit.

The outro before it recasts the intro's page in the morning: the strip light off, the doorway quiet, and the count, "one, two, three", now the family's handprints from the fridge catching the sun one by one (sunlit tiles "don't read as sunlit tiles"). On the last beats, the child's footsteps come down the side of the page to the kitchen doorway.

I quite like the book result, I think we've said enough by this point that a flash back through it all fits the music nicely.

Sessions 8-10 · 7-13 October

After the first cut

The whole song was built by the end of session 7, and the first complete cut rendered overnight. Watching it end to end showed what working one section at a time had hidden: consistency lives between drawings, and nothing had compared them since verse 1.

I've been rather fussy about consistency but I bet I've still missed loads of places. Rather than start fixing this right away, I'd like to look at the pattern and see if we can build this into our process.

So reviewing got its own tools: a player that saves each note with the song time, the sung line and the drawings on screen, and continuity sheets that crop every prop out of every drawing that shows it, side by side under the bible's words. The faults went on one list (a spatula on the wrong side of the cooker, a socket on the wrong wall, the breathing card drawn as a circle on the master plate) and each was fixed in every drawing at once, with the misreading written into the bible as something the prop must never be.

When the film was ready to go up on YouTube, a rewatch still found a slip in the one detail the whole film is most careful about.

I just watched this video again and realised we made a howler! Well... two actually but on the same sequence.

His fist in chorus 1 read as a right hand, with the band on the wrong finger. Redrawn as his left, it came back with the band on his middle finger in both takes, and it passed our check: seen from above, the top two knuckles of a fist read as one. The next cut went out with it.

I'm afraid his ring is still on the wrong finger when he has a fist at 1:09 though. Let's update our process to make sure I double-check before we go to the trouble of releasing.

The dawn's wide shot needed two more rounds. Its first drawing gave the two of them three hands and sank his face into her head; the prompt had asked for his right arm round her shoulders while she sat on his left, an arm that would have to cross his whole body. Redrawn with two hands, his face still went too deep:

I still feel like his face is buried too deep into where her head would be, it looks off

Even the clean-up left marks. A patch meant to take the kettle's old lead away only half-erased it, and every light of the dawn inherited the broken loop ("the kettle lead has gone wonky in those light renders"); it was painted out by hand. From then on, every round of fixes went back to be watched before anything was released.

Looking back

What worked, what didn't

Worked

  • Timings first, locked by ear. Every word's moment was settled before any art existed, and everything keys off it.
  • One curve for the whole film. The unease curve gave it a build, a peak and a stillness.
  • Layout, then redraw. Our camera, Codex's hand. Codex keeps a layout's composition almost exactly.
  • The room bible. Fifty-odd drawings, one kitchen: the same tap, fridge, mugs, rings and coats throughout.
  • Patching instead of redrawing, and drawings derived from approved ones, so dissolves are exact.
  • Light and words as code. The count and fizz, the call dropping, the dawn, the steam writing: the drawings stay still while the light moves and the words arrive on time.
  • Options side by side whenever words failed: three ways to whisper a line, four skins, the warp at full and half.
  • One section per session, each a test with sound, a commit and a written handoff.

Didn't, or not yet

  • The 3D film. Hard to steer in words; it left behind the set, the count and fizz, and the perception layer.
  • Free drawings. Alive, and a different room every time.
  • The print stipple. Painted on rather than part of the drawing. Parked.
  • The full-strength warp. It melted the drawings; softened above a knee.
  • Early story placement. The homecoming and their first touch were built too early and saved for the end; the storyboard's last shot was spent before the ending.
  • Codex and hands. Rings on the wrong finger, an extra digit, nails on palms, skin like a rash: every hand checked by counting from the thumb.
  • Checking our own work. A band on the wrong finger passed two checks and went out in a cut; now every round of fixes is watched before anything is released.

How it's built, end to end:

  1. The song as data: a beat grid from the MIDI, levels and hits from the stems, every word's time from Whisper and the lyrics, confirmed by ear; who sings what, by pitch.
  2. A story and its rules: no faces; every lyric on screen at its moment; his voice cold, hers warm.
  3. The room bible: every prop and person in one file, blocked into a Blender set built by a script.
  4. For each panel, a layout render from the set, redrawn by Codex against the master plate and reference sheets, checked at zoom, patched where needed.
  5. An animator in Python that composes each frame on the music: panels, captions, balloons, handwriting, sound effects, light, and a perception layer that follows the unease curve.
  6. A test with sound for each section, notes, rounds, approval; then the full cut, continuity sheets across every drawing, and a last check of every fix before release.

The method, the prompts and the lessons are written up for the next song in a music video kit: a Claude Code skill (the playbook, every Codex prompt, the checklists) and the tools that aren't tied to this song, with a command that starts a new project from them and keeps its copies in step.
