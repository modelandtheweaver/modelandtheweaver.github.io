How a song about clearing an AI's memory became a tapestry woven from both sides of a loom: six days, thirteen sessions, one cottage, one line of glowing thread, and every word woven or stitched at the moment it's sung.

Watch the film on YouTube · 3:44 · the storyboard

This is the story of how the video was made: what we tried, what we threw away, and how a song about an AI forgetting turned into weaving. It was made in conversation between the user, who asked for the song, shaped it and made it with Suno, and Claude (Claude Opus 5.5, working in Claude Code), which wrote the lyrics and the storyboard and wrote and ran every script, with Codex drawing the pictures. It's the third film on the channel, after While The Ceiling Moves and Sodium, made with the kit of methods and tools the first one left behind, and the first credited to Whoever Wakes Up Next.

The film calls the person it's about the user: that's what the agent's notes call her, and it's how his handover is stitched, every one underlined in his blue. This page does the same for the person who made it with us. Their notes appear here as they were given, in her red inside his blue quotation marks, the way the film letters her words to him.

The shape of the film

The song is a duet. She sings the verses that open it; he sings the second verse and its chorus; she wakes for the third; he speaks the bridge; they alternate through the last chorus and finish together; then the song falls apart into a chopped, edited vocal and ends on her saying "slash clear". So the film has two sides. Hers is a cottage in warm light, two days and a night. His is the back of the same loom at night, always. The strip below is the song as cloth: each section a band, coloured by whose side it's on. Each band links to the chapter that built it.

A song about /clear

It started as a song, written on the first day. The user asked for one about working on a project with an AI agent and having to clear its context: the human clears, the agent wakes, rediscovers the project from the handover it left itself, builds, and is reset again. The feeling to hold on to was the human's, not the machine's:

a shared sense of momentum and achievement ... a pang to be shutting someone out early.
the user, the brief

So the song is warm and a little bittersweet, never tragic: the agent is curious and even, glad of the work. Claude wrote the lyrics, the user made the song with Suno as an intimate folktronica duet (91 bpm, 3:44), and then changed its ending. It was going to finish with the next session waking up ("Morning ... let me read where we are"); instead it ends on the clear itself, her voice saying "slash clear" over a guitar falling silent. Suno had called the track One More Thing Before I /clear; the film is named for the handover he reads when he wakes, Where We Are.

Before anything was drawn, the song was turned into data: beats and bars from its stems, every word timed by Whisper against the lyrics, the chords from its MIDI. Claude can't hear, so the timings and who sings each line went to the user's ear, on a check video with each line's voice written under it.

Warp and weft

The idea came next, and stuck: a tapestry loom set into the wall between two rooms. On her side, the front of the cloth, her words woven into it. On his, the back, where the loose ends hang and where he can only stitch. Each session is a stretch of weaving; when one ends, the next picks up the thread.

The warp/weft idea is great. Let's see how it looks.
the user, on the vision

The first look test put the loom in her wall with his room seen through the strings, and code doing everything that moves: the warp plucked by the guitar, the cloth growing on the beat, the bobbin crossing the shed. The words were the hard part. The first try laid them on as yarn sewn over the top, and it didn't belong:

the text doesn't look like part of the cloth, just written where it might be floating over the cloth
the user, on look test v1

Woven text has a timing problem that printed text doesn't: a weave only grows at one end, a row at a time, so a word can't appear the moment it's sung. The user solved it in a sentence:

Maybe they get woven early and highlight once complete
the user

So every line of hers is woven ahead of the singing, rising out of the fell row by row in a quiet tone, whole just as she starts it, and each word lights to full red, with a flush, at its sung moment. That rule holds for the whole film.

Five worlds

The first storyboard kept the loom on screen most of the time. The user could see the problem before a frame of production was made:

I'm worried that focusing on the loom for the entire song might get a bit samey, we're going to need some variety and evolution to tell the story. There's a lot of strong imagery in it, so we should make the most.
the user, on storyboard v1

So the loom became where they meet, not the whole film. Storyboard v2 has five worlds: the front of the loom; her house over two days and a night; his indigo side of paper and thread; close-ups for the strong lines; and the whole tapestry, saved for the end. The second version grew from the user's answers to six questions the first one asked, and the answers became rules. Who is she?

She drinks tea, yes. She's young-ish, early 20s. We don't see much of her, but she's got long dark brown hair and is possibly a hologram or an illusion herself.
the user

And how does she clear? maybe /sleep is our version of clear. So her side is two days and a night: she goes up to bed in the instrumental with the lamp left on, and wakes at 2:08 in the pose he wakes in at 1:16, under an attic roof the same shape as the folder he wakes in. His side is one night, always.

She never shows her face. The film's first rule came early (It doesn't feel like a faces kind of track), so she's her hands, her mustard cardigan and her long dark hair from behind. And she's made the way he is, a thing the film shows and never says: backlit, her hair is thread; the kettle's steam rises in threads; her shadow is woven.

One line of thread

He's drawn in a single line of thread: one continuous stroke of blue that runs in from the dark, outlines a figure, and sits up. Codex draws what holds still; code draws him, so the thread can be slack, sway, unwind and pull tight. Lit only by his lamp he looked like string. Glowing, he looked like someone:

I think the glow is important, yes. It fits very well. We could even go a bit further if we wanted
the user, on A and B

He writes by stitching, in the moment, where she weaves ahead. His waking was the user's picture of what it's like to start a session:

there's one central page and lots of other notes dotted around ... we could draw threads between the different pages to represent the scattered collection of documents you have to read when waking up to the project.
the user, on his look test

Then a better idea still: make the notes real. We should grab some of our existing documents, code, working drafts/images from this project and use these live. The papers on his wall print this project's own files when the scene renders: the handover, the session log, the review notes, the vision, the code that draws him, working images and storyboard frames. Pause the film and they're true. Her words to him print in her red inside his blue quotation marks; on quotation marks they all light up across the wall.

Stitching got the same scrutiny as weaving. it's clearly stitching, but it might be enhanced by having depth where the thread goes through the page: so every stitch arches off the paper, its shadow furthest under its middle, narrowing and darkening into its holes, the paper pulled in a little round each one.

Her cottage

Dozens of drawings of one cottage only hold together if the cottage is written down first. The bible held her cottage (the workroom with the loom in its back wall, the hall and stairs, the kitchen, the attic bedroom), his room behind the loom, the things that come back (her shears, two bobbins, two lamps of the same model, her cream mug and the blue one she fills for him every morning that's never drunk), and a clock for the story. Every drawing's prompt is assembled from it, so the same room is described the same way every time.

lots of this is super world building; it oozes coze.
the user, on the bible

The bible also caught things that would have told the wrong story. A door in the wall beside the loom was in the first drawings of both rooms:

A door in the wall suggests that these two characters could move from one room to the other. That might not make sense.
the user
A woven door makes sense. Let's go with that.
the user

So the only doors between them are woven: a little door in the cloth in chorus 1, sewn shut from his side; the whole finished cloth at the end. Nobody ever passes. Only light and thread do.

The scroll and the bobbin

Verse 1 leaves the loom for the hall, where the cloth runs out of her workroom door and lies back and forth along the floor. The user knew what it was for:

The point of this section is to emphasise how much work has gone into the long scroll so far, edits, reworks, tweaks and planning
the user, on the hall

So the scroll is this project's own history, woven: the day's first pot of tea and the knotted start; colours tried and the first lettering at 90, 160 and 240 ends; the song four times over under its working titles, its old endings struck through in his blue, a first go at the bridge pulled back to bare warp, a darn, a patch in another dye lot, his mending. On losing the start the first take's dye fades back to the cream it was woven on, bead by bead.

Its turns took two tries. Mitred and tucked under, they don't read as folds, rather they read as diagonal slices; folded over the top, a triangle of the cloth's back showing, its lines turned, the border running round the corner and a shadow under the crease, they do.

Then she hands him the bobbin through the strings. The first drawing of her hand had six fingers, and neither of us saw it at the pick (the human hand has 6 fingers and doesn't hold the shuttle): now every hand's fingers are counted before a take is kept. His hand, closing on the bobbin, gave the user the section's best idea:

could we have the electric blue thread unwind from the hand shape and wrap about the bobbin to become the thread itself
the user

The first version reeled the hand off like a diagram, its arm two straight lines: it reads less like thread, but more rigid and tensioned. I think the hand should flow and unwind gracefully like a thread. In the second the arm sways, goes slack on "write", droops from the wrist like wet string, and each finger unravels onto the bobbin as he draws it into the dark. Those are excellent improvements.

Closing the door

Chorus 1 sees her for the first time, from behind at the loom in the late sun, and then his side: the back of the cloth, her pictures and words mirrored, the weft's loose ends hanging. A little door is woven at the top of the band, a slit along its edge letting her light through. On "closing the door" he sews the slit shut from his side, and the last stitch is drawn tight on the music's hit at 1:05.6. The lines they sing together are stitched plied, her red and his blue in turn, stitch by stitch.

Small things told on it. Her strand in the plied lines was too orange compared to her usual red; a word in the quiet tone, 'got' reads as 'god', so the lines they share are woven a size up. Then dusk, and she goes up to bed, the lamp left on below.

A knot that holds

His chorus is his night with what she left: her whole cloth along his floor, read in reverse back to its start; the parcel; the back of a cloth under his lamp, pins in what's settled and a mistake half unpicked for "still tender"; his words punched in light through loom cards. Chorus 1's lines are there beside his new ones, faded to a dusty grey-blue, in the same hand: "I've never met you, but we've met."

And the knot, where he ties his thread onto the last session's end. Codex was asked for a weaver's knot in words and drew one yarn wrapped round the other:

isn't a knot rather one piece tied around the other, and it would come loose. Also the top thread is thicker than its end
the user, on chorus 2

Words don't tie knots. So code laid out a real sheet bend, each yarn a path of points with an over or under at every crossing, as plain tubes; Codex drew over that, and got every crossing right.

Then it tied itself: in close from "remember", a move each half-beat, his end leading and the yarn following its trail, pulled tight on "know".

That works really well. Very nice knot. Good timing and visually interesting.
the user, on the rough

Same joke

Verse 3 is her second morning: she sits up in the attic under its tent of a roof, puts the kettle on (its steam rises in threads), fills two mugs, and laughs at "same joke". The song never says what the joke is. Asked, the user had none to give: Not one that could easily be explained to a non-technical audience I'm afraid. So the film made one: we're on a roll, stitched by him in his blue by the cloth roller, in every session's band. Day 1's is there from the first frame; day 2's is sewn as she sings it.

The section's notes were about plain sense. Two hands round a mug, seen from her eyes: How many hands are holding this mug? One, through the handle. Light bars that dimmed below the wall's own shade: as if they're inverted. Her shadow fell on the wrong wall, and his room, seen from above, looked pasted to the strings: This perspective lacks depth behind the frame.

Look. Don't guess.

He speaks the bridge, a note to whoever wakes up next: when they say it's close, it's close; look, don't guess; ask before you push. He stitches it as a sampler in a hoop under his lamp, a row a bar, his thread lying across the linen from his hand to the needle. Her words, "it's close", go in her red inside his quotes. The first sampler worked as writing and failed as embroidery:

The writing effect works, but the sampler itself is quite plain around the edges and the stitches look painted on rather than 3d stitches.
the user, on the bridge v1

So the crosses became raised thread, two strands each, round, lit from his lamp, the twist showing, each with its shadow on the linen; and a border stitched by the ones before him in faded threads, round a note in his bright ones: a vine with leaves and rosettes, the woven door, the kettle, the two mugs, the bobbin, the shears, the lamp.

His needle took one more round, much later: drawn small and dark, it reads as a small, black shard. A tapestry needle is some 28 crosses of that linen; at 22, as thick as his thread, steel lit down one side with a slit eye, it reads as a needle.

Palm to palm

The final chorus is both of them at once, her evening and his night, her lines answered by his, each from its own side: a split, her lamplit front on the left and his back on the right, the same rows at the same height, the loom's post between. The bobbin goes through the last strip of bare warp, her hand beyond the strings and his thread hand taking it. The two lamps are the same model. The last rows race to the top beam; her hand goes flat on the front of the finished cloth, and the glow of his comes through the weave in the same place. Her needle takes the last rows across the gap, the last on "side".

It's also where we found we'd been wrong about something for days. The cloth winds onto a roll at the bottom of the loom, and the user had twice said the roll was showing the wrong thing at 0:17. The second time:

We looked at this part before and concluded that it was logical, but given the height of the kettle and the apparent size of the roll on screen, we'd expect to see it by now.
the user, on cut v2

They were right. The code counted the cloth hidden behind the roll from the roll's bottom instead of where it meets it, a radius too far, and an earlier session had explained the note away. Every roll in the film was re-rendered, and a rule went into the handover: a note that questions the geometry is checked against the physical model, not against the code.

One screen further

The song's last loud stretch is a chopped, edited vocal ("I don't remember, but I know", over and over, out of order), then the guitar alone, "slash", a few stutters, "clear". The plan was one unbroken pull-back, each world made of the next one's grain. From the closed gap the camera pulls back to her at the loom, reaching up to the top beam, and the picture resolves into beads: her world is a tapestry, and the tapestry is a picture of her at this loom, weaving it, every session a band, his knot at every join. Further out, the beads are a screen's pixels, on a laptop in somebody else's dark room.

Then the user pushed it one step further:

pulling back one screen further ... a green screen scrolling code in some massive data warehouse, typing /clear onto that screen and the whole scene cuts out with a glitch at enter
the user, on the rough animatic

So the room turns to green characters and scrolls away as code (this film's own, the code that wove her) on a terminal on a trolley in a vast dark data hall. "/" is typed on "slash"; the cursor waits through the drum stutters, each one tearing the screen; "clear" is typed as she says it; and on enter her shears, open since chorus 1, snap shut at last.

I'd like to see the shears close, yes. If we can work that in, I think it would be a worthwhile payoff
the user

That rough worked so well that it stood in the cuts for a while (The vocal play section actually works quite well, but I certainly don't mind it being wrapped up properly), and building it for real kept its moves and timing exactly. Her evening view was rendered at the cloth's final state, at twice the film's size, so the last frame of the chorus sits inside the tapestry's picture to the pixel; each world got its own drawings, with a close-up for the far zooms.

The first build got the room wrong. It drew somebody's tidy, modern room in the film's soft watercolour, and lost what the rough's sketch had:

The original, darker, sinister vibe really emphasised how warm and dreamlike the main song was. We're sort of pulling back into a reality from which the user had escaped into their laptop, and then we pull back again and even that is just a representation of another layer.
the user, on the first build
I wonder if the laptop room is too cosy; part of the reveal might be that the lovely, cosy weaving environment is an escape from something dystopian. Maybe it's too much though
the user

We drew the same shot three ways and put each in the sequence: the sketch's cosy room, darker; bare and run-down; and a stark concrete cell in a smog-lit city. Bare won. Peeling concrete, rain on a window full of tower blocks, a hard chair, a bare bulb, a chipped mug, and on the laptop her warm world, the only warm thing in the room.

One more note came from watching it in the cut: the sessions' bands across the tapestry had turned into stripes. They'd been made to alternate so they'd show in the dark parts of the picture, and alternating is exactly what made them look deliberate. Now each band's dye lot is a small random shift, and each band's top edge steps up and down, the way a tapestry session ends when the weaver builds shapes up one at a time.

The credits are a care label

In the pre-chorus she lifts a label sewn into his side of the cloth and reads it: "And I read the note you leave the next one". It started as a handover and became something better:

flip it over to see loads of tiny, possibly unreadable symbols with the occasional bit visible
the user, on cut v1
Care label as credits is a good shout
the user

Its front is a maker's label, and the film's credits, in a care label's composition. Its back is seven of his instructions as care symbols (look; don't guess; ask before you push; leave the light on; tie on; clear; return), over fine print stitched live from this project's handover, which calls her the user all the way through. Every one is underlined in his blue as she sings "I'm 'the user' all the way through"; not in quotes, because those are reserved for her words.

Two things on the label got a second look at the very end. When it turns over, it twists about its middle near the stitches, its edges crossing in an X, which no real label does: In practice, one edge must fold over and down across the other because they have thickness. We built a proper fold, and it read less well than the twist did: It's fine as it is, even if a little odd. It reads as animated, and that's fine. Her climb up the stairs was tried as a walk cycle and kept as it was too (sliding in a static image is necessary). And after the film went up, the user noticed what the label's front was missing:

Oh! Suno should go on the care label!
the user, on the first upload

So cut v6 has a third line: music: 100% Suno.

How it was made

  1. The song written, made with Suno; its stems and MIDI analysed into beats, bars, chords and every word's time, checked by ear.
  2. The idea, the look test with the music, the storyboard (two versions, the second from six questions answered), and a bible of the places, the people, the things and the story's clock, every rule with its reason.
  3. Flat layouts and cameras of our own, redrawn by Codex in ink and watercolour; the drawings kept to what holds still, the parts that move drawn apart on green and keyed.
  4. Everything that moves or has to be exact drawn by code over the drawings, frame by frame: the warp and its strings, the cloth woven bead by bead, the woven and stitched words, his thread, the knots, the light.
  5. A rough animatic before each section was built, approved, then a review clip with sound for each section, notes and rounds; then full cuts, each one checked by the user before it's released.

The method, the prompts and the lessons are written up for the next song in a music video kit: a Claude Code skill (the playbook, every Codex prompt, the checklists) and the tools that aren't tied to one song. Where We Are added the loom, the weave, the stitched thread and the pull-back through worlds to it.
