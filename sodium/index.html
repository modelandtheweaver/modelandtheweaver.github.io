A making-of · a music video for Amber & Ink · 8–14 October 2026

SodiumAmber & Ink

How a garage track about distance became a woman's walk home at 3am through a real English town: seven days, ten sessions, twelve sets built from maps and measurements, and a story rebuilt after its first viewer.

Watch the film on YouTube · 4:08

This is the story of how the video was made: what we tried first, what we threw away, and how a walk home became the whole film. It was made in conversation between , who wrote the song and made it with Suno, and Claude (Claude Opus 5.5, working in Claude Code), which wrote and ran every script, with Codex drawing the pictures. It's the second film credited to Amber & Ink, after While The Ceiling Moves, and it started from the method and tools that film left behind. The shape of the film

Forty-eight minutes in four

Sodium is one walk: 3.7 km from the care home where Amber works nights to the terrace she shares with Ink, on real streets, from 02:58 to 03:46 by the story's clock. That's forty-eight minutes of walking in four minutes of song, so each section of the song is a stretch of the route. The line below is how far she has walked. It climbs through the verses and choruses and stops twice: at the lights, where the film cuts to Ink standing on his own doorstep, and on the bridge, where they meet. It ends at their door. Underneath are the sessions that built each section. Each band links to its part of the story.

Session 1 · 8 October, 9:50 pm

The brief

I've put a new song into this folder and wanted to see how we get on. The skill is currently based on a comic-book style of animation. I'd like to see how we can take this approach and grow it to produce some new and interesting effect that match the song … go cautiously on codex because the results look very AI at times, and consistency took a lot of our time
, the first message

The song came from Suno as Bridge Between Us: four minutes and three seconds of two-step UK garage at 149.5 beats a minute (it feels like 75), in D♭ major, on one four-chord loop almost all the way through, G♭, D♭, A♭, B♭ minor. It ends on the minor chord, not home. It's about two people apart, "the lines, the miles, the time apart", building back to each other word by word, and its motif is light: "two shadows underneath one light", "that little light that says we're not that far".

The first job was the one While The Ceiling Moves had taught us: turn the song into data, because Claude can't listen to it. The kit's tools did most of that (a beat grid from the MIDI, levels and hits from each stem, every word's time from Whisper matched against the lyrics), and this song found their weak spots. Whisper heard none of the choruses at first: its voice detector had thrown the processed chorus vocals away. Without it, and with a second pass that prompts Whisper a few lines at a time, 306 of the 325 sung words matched. The lyric check video went to 's ear twice ("That's much improved … it's pretty close"), and every fix to the tools went back into the kit for the next song.

Session 1 · the first evening

A bridge made of words

The first proposal took the old title literally. A river at night with a lamp on each bank, and in chorus 1 every sung word dropping in as a carved stone of an arch at the moment it's sung, the echoes laid from the far bank, the cracks between them filled with gold. It came in two looks: A rendered entirely in code, B the same stones over a river Codex drew from our layout.

That looks good, and plays to the ideas in the song well. The main problem is that the only vocalist here is Amber, she's backing herself so the bridge building from the otherside doesn't quite land right. It's a song to Ink, but he's not singing.
, on looks A and B

That one observation set a rule for the whole film: nothing in it may be built as Ink's answer from the other side. The bracketed echoes in the lyrics are her own voice, double-tracked. Look B was "the stronger for the detail in the cityscape", but one scene couldn't tell a story, and wanted one: "something punchy and surprising".

Session 1 · later that night

Her picture

Claude offered twists: the other side of the first film's night, a pirate radio station, the song as the bridge itself. The story came from a picture already had:

a sort of top-down angle on a lone figure walking along beside a streetlight, looking down at their phone, casting a shadow behind and then ahead as they pass under the light, still comic-book panel style. She's texting, it's late and a little drizzly. She's remembering the last time she walked together with Ink, and how she's struggling with his insecurities and anxiety disorder … I'm not sure entirely what the story is here, to be honest. It's fun to discover it.

The story grew from it. Amber, a night carer, walks home from the late shift in the drizzle under sodium streetlights, texting Ink, who is awake at home with his anxiety. Her call drops on a footbridge over the river. A second shadow stretches into her light from the dark, carrying an umbrella: Ink has come out to meet her halfway. A storyboard of sixteen Codex sketches went over the same night.

Read that, loved it. Listened and imagined along. I think it's super … Ink's arrival is lovely. It's almost a reflected version of the original story. She's the vulnerable one, and he's there for her. They're supporting each other.

The questions it raised were answered one at a time over the next days, and each became a rule. It's the same Amber as in While The Ceiling Moves, earlier in their relationship: their first rented home, no rings yet, no baby things. (Her singing voice is British here and country and western in the first film; in her backstory she's good at accents.) It happens at about 3am at midsummer, the shortest night, so dawn at their door is true. Her coat is an ochre rain mac: Amber wears amber, Ink wears ink. Still no faces. And the song was renamed:

Bridge Between Us is nauseatingly obvious and missing an opportunity to do something more interesting.

Of the titles offered, took Sodium, the light the whole film is drawn in: "I think the song is called Sodium. I never liked Bridge Between Us much."

Session 2 · 9 October

Comic, not animation

The first look test animated the storyboard's walk. A street of terraced houses was laid out in metres and redrawn by Codex panel by panel, and on top of it, all computed, Amber walked one lamp every four bars with a step on every beat, her shadow from each lamp swinging round her like a clock hand as she passed under it. The lyrics were painted on the road like road markings, and her echoes were written inside her own shadow.

Although it's quite cute and charming, it doesn't have the comic-book vibe we're going for. Rather than attempt to animate the walking frame-by-frame, we should focus on using panels, swipes, fades, etc, to give the drawn-but-animated feel. I'm concerned that we'll spend a lot of time trying to get this motion to look natural rather than computer game-y.
, on the walk

So the film became held drawings with the motion in the page: panels sliding in on the beat, the camera pulling back across a page, pushes into a detail, lettering arriving as it's sung. The first comic pages went up that evening, and eleven notes came back. Grouped by cause before any was fixed, they came down to five, and each got a check that runs before every render from then on: lettering that doesn't sit on its surface, lettering that doesn't look like the real thing, things that couldn't happen in a real street, a street that looks generated, and which lyric goes where.

Session 3 · 9 October

Ways of seeing

I'd also like a conversation about using parallax effects to create depth in scenes. We want to emphasise distance and space, emptiness in a cluttered scene. We can also use CCTV pictures and other effects to add interest, and a slightly sinister undertone.

The conversation was held over real output. A small depth model, Depth Anything V2, running on the CPU, gave every drawing a depth map, and a parallax camera moved through it. A town camera's picture was built in code the way a cheap recorder keeps it: an optical zoom that hunts for focus, a few frames a second, monochrome, grain, a timestamp, and a wall of four monitors with VIDEO LOSS on one. Then a convex traffic mirror, a shop's shutter as a wipe, and her phone's map.

The use of parallax and cctv definitely have a place here, I wonder what other perspectives we could have. Keeping a simple journey home interesting is all about the presentation and interesting angles, observations and clever use of the set.

That became the film's structure: a way of seeing for each section. Her own eyes on her phone in verse 1. The city's cameras in chorus 1. Traffic lights changing for nobody in the first instrumental. Shop windows and reflections in verse 2. Her map in chorus 2. The lamps failing around her before the bridge. And two witnesses from the list of ideas: "the fox would be great, and someone's doorbell is an excellent idea." The fox is the one other thing awake, and it turns up three times. The doorbell became their own, and it ends the film.

Sessions 3-5 · 9-10 October

A real town

Her map needed streets, so it got a real town: Bedford, from OpenStreetMap, never named on screen. Its Victorian suspension footbridge of 1888, white lattice arches over the Great Ouse, was already the storyboard's bridge. The first route was forced through the places the story wanted, and it showed: she walked round a roundabout's carriageway, and Ink took the long way round like a car. spotted each one. So the route was computed the way someone on foot would walk it, with only the bridge as a waypoint, and the streets chose the rest: a long terrace, a footpath between garden walls, the lights on The Broadway, the High Street, the Embankment, the bridge, Mill Meadows, the Rope Walk zebra, home. The song's sections landed on it without forcing.

Then photographs. Geograph's archive answers searches by position, so a script walked her route every 100 metres and kept what was near it: 238 photographs, of which 45 were kept. The find was N Chadwick's night series from 2016 of Foster Hill Road and the High Street under the old low-pressure sodium lamps, everything one orange: her streets in the film's own light. Mapillary's dashcam frames filled the gaps nobody had photographed, like the zebra on Roff Avenue. All of them were references, never traced: every street in the film is our own set, drawn by Codex, and every photographer is credited in the video's description.

Fine to include them; interesting to have a real place behind this. I like that someone who knows would know.
, on Bedford's landmarks

So John Bunyan's statue stands at the lights, the steel faces meet nose to nose up Silver Street, and the bridge is the real one, restaged. It has no lamps on its deck, so the lamps that fail are the Embankment's, and one lamp post at the far end throws his shadow. Every change to the real place is on a list of liberties, from a lamp every two houses (for the timing of her shadows) to the fox's winter coat at midsummer, and each is drawn the same way in every panel, so it reads as the place and never as a slip.

I don't mind artistic license, if we're careful about it not looking like a generative mistake.

Sessions 4-5 · 10 October

Sets, because words weren't enough

While The Ceiling Moves was one kitchen, built once in Blender and redrawn by Codex from our camera. Sodium is a whole route through a town, and it needed the same treatment for the same reason: asked in words, Codex can't place a street lamp.

Yes, A looks correct. But we must make sure that they are on the correct side of the pavement and facing in the right direction. Codex gets them all over the place, sometimes nearside, sometimes far.
, picking the town's lamp

From words alone it stood the column in the middle of the pavement, pointed the neck along the street, moved the lamp across the road and laid it flat from above. Drawn from a set's layout, every lamp stayed at the kerb with its neck out over the road.

So twelve sets were built, each by a script from real measurements: the bridge (a 30.5 m span, a 1.83 m deck rising 1.9 m to its crown, rods every 1.9 m), Gladstone Street (fronts 14.5 m apart by OpenStreetMap's buildings, its houses checked against a photograph from the photographer's own position), Roff Avenue's zebra, Foster Hill Road, The Broadway's lights, the High Street, the ward, the Embankment, the Rope Walk zebra, Turner Way and their own close, and a stranger's terrace that the rework later cut. A set renders a layout for Codex, a depth map for parallax, masks of every part and light passes for our own lighting. A seeded generator gives every house its own door, windows, alarm box and bins, because the real street is mirrored pairs of cottages and its variety is all in the details.

Sets also catch what a drawing hides. Seen from the side, Amber stands behind the bridge's near railing, a rod and the lattice. That's right in 3D, and Codex merged her into the ironwork.

Good idea building a model for the bridge. Codex had parts of her body mixed into the railings.

Now any view that sees someone through ironwork is drawn empty and the people go in afterwards: Codex draws the person alone on a green ground in the set's pose, and the set's masks decide which rails pass in front of them.

Lettering · sessions 5-9

Words in the street

Every sung line is on screen once, in a form chosen for what it means, and most are lettered onto real things in the street: a road sign's name plate (THE IN-BETWEEN), a lost-cat poster with WE added in marker, a bus shelter's advert for an invented phone network, relay ("Never out of reach."), with line 4 as its headline, a chemist's green LED cross, a departure board reading NO MORE BUSES TONIGHT · FIRST BUS 05:42, grit-dusted tar in the road's cracks, a memorial bench's brass plaque, the planks of the bridge and the stripes of a zebra. Each is designed as its real object first (a British road sign's typeface and border, a home printer's poster, a phone's chat with its ticks) and laid onto the drawing by code, on corners fitted to the drawn edges.

Each surface is checked on a sheet at twice the size before any render, and 's eye still caught what the sheet passed. The cross first scrolled the whole line across its LEDs:

Idea is good, font is hard to read especially at speed

Looking back

What worked, what didn't

Worked

  • A set before every drawing. Twelve sets from real measurements kept the lamps at the kerb, the people out of the railings and the shadows true, and gave every drawing its depth and masks.
  • A real place. OpenStreetMap for the route and the sets, photographs for the look, and the liberties on a list.
  • A way of seeing per section. Cameras, a doorbell, a convex mirror, a map, a reflection: the walk keeps changing how it's shown.
  • Lettering on real objects, each designed as the real thing and checked on a sheet before any render.
  • Light as code. Lamps failing in walking order and restriking pink to amber, a shadow cast a frame at a time, the first light: the drawings stay still while the light moves.
  • The first film's method and tools, carried over in the kit, and improved there as we went.
  • Rendering only what changed. Each cut after the first is the last cut's frames with the changed spans re-rendered.

Didn't, or not yet

  • The words as a bridge. A lovely idea that gave Ink a voice the song doesn't have.
  • Animated walking. Charming and "computer game-y"; replaced by the page.
  • Codex from words, for anything placed. Lamps, a bus's seats, a reflection, an umbrella on a lawn: each needed a set or code.
  • The story, the first time. It showed what happened and never said it, and a first viewer read the opposite. Real messages and dramatic irony fixed it.
  • The map. Liked, and too much: about 75 seconds of phone came down to a few glimpses.
  • Reading every note. A cut was released over the notes on it. Now the tools refuse.

How it's built, end to end:

  1. The song as data: a beat grid from the MIDI, levels and hits from the stems, every word's time from Whisper and the lyrics, confirmed by ear.
  2. A story and its rules, a storyboard, and the whole film planned on a real route: each section's stretch of the walk, its way of seeing and every line's form.
  3. A bible of the people and places, and a set for each place, built by a script from maps, photographs and measurements.
  4. For each panel, a layout from the set redrawn by Codex against the bible's sheets, checked at zoom, patched where needed; people put in from their own drawings where ironwork or glass comes between.
  5. An animator in Python that composes each frame on the music: the page and its camera, the panels, the lettering on its surfaces, captions, balloons, the cameras' pictures, light, rain and shadows.
